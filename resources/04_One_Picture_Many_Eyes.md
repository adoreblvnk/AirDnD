## §1 THE SITUATION

Everything that could hurt Singapore arrives from beyond our borders - across the sea approaches, or through a handful of air corridors - into a country fifty kilometres wide. There is no depth here, no rear area and no second chance - warning time is the only defence we have, and it is measured in minutes. The information that would buy those minutes already exists: radars, cameras, transponders, shipping data, flight logs, and the ordinary traffic of one of the world's busiest sea lanes. It sits in a dozen systems that disagree about identity, about time, and about how far each other should be  believed,  and  nobody  has  joined  it.  Collection  stopped  being  the  problem  years ago. We are drowning in signals and blind anyway.

## P R O B L E M   S T A T E M E N T

Ideate a way to fuse sources nobody has joined - sensors, open data, and things that were never meant to be sensors - into usable warning, either that a conflict is forming or of what is on our approaches now. Showcase a software demo that turns many disagreeing inputs into one picture somebody can act on.

## §3 TWO EXAMPLES

## O N E   -   S E E I N G   I T   C O M I N G ,   F R O M   O P E N   S O U R C E S

VIEWS,  run  by  a  Norwegian  peace  research  institute  and  a  Swedish  university, publishes  monthly  machine-learning  forecasts  of  armed  conflict  one  to  thirty-six months ahead worldwide, and identified seven of the ten deadliest countries in 2024. It  can  be  downloaded.  Separately,  people  posting  online  behave  as  a  distributed sensor  network:  untrained  people  photograph  what  they  should  not,  and  official phrasing  moving  in  lockstep  across  nominally  independent  channels  indicates  an order was issued. Automated tools geolocate video from terrain and shadow angles to within a few metres. The open ground is between those two scales, and nothing joins them.

## T W O   -   A   B R A I N   T H A T   I N F E R S   A C R O S S   E V E R Y T H I N G

One system,  modular  interfaces,  any  input,  a  comprehensive  picture  out.  Anduril's Lattice does this; NATO selected it for next-generation air command and control and the US Army has committed up to twenty billion dollars. The instructive detail is an exercise in which it linked F-16s, surface-to-air launchers, MQ-9 Reapers and Army howitzers into a single network, none of which Anduril built. The value lay entirely in integrating other people's equipment.
